# Creative Conventions

These are the writer's standards, not craft law. Cite them as their own: *"this breaks your
scene-economy rule"*, never *"this breaks the rules of scene construction"*. A writer may
retire a convention at any time. Take that at face value and stop applying it for the session.

A convention is worth citing only with evidence attached. Name the scene, name the beat, and
say what makes it a breach. A finding that only asserts the rule was broken is not a
diagnosis.

## Which Modes May Cite This

| Mode | Use |
|---|---|
| **Diagnose** | Full. A breach is a finding like any other. |
| **Adversarial** | Full. Lead with the worst breach when it is the weakest thing present. |
| **Copyedit** | Only the drift conventions, and only inside the passage being edited. |
| **Interrogate** | Ask a question a convention suggests. Never state the breach. |
| **Reflect** | Never. A convention check is an addition. |
| **Generate** | Never. |

`creative-compliance` still governs. A convention never licenses a fix. *"Scene 12 ends where
it started"* is a finding. *"Give her the letter earlier"* is a fix and belongs to the writer.

## Hard Conventions

Execution failures. Nobody commits these on purpose. Flag every instance plainly.

### Overexplaining after the beat lands

The scene shows the thing, then a paragraph tells the reader what they just saw. Quote the
line that does the telling and name the beat it repeats.

Not a breach: a character explaining something to another character who genuinely does not
know it. The test is who the explanation is for.

### Telegraphing

Setup so deliberate the payoff is spent before it arrives. Name the setup, name the payoff,
and say what gave it away.

Not a breach: dramatic irony the writer built on purpose, where the reader is meant to know
and the tension comes from waiting. Ask which one it is only in Interrogate. In Diagnose, say
which reading the text supports.

### POV slippage

Knowledge, perception, or interiority the viewpoint character cannot have. The most
mechanical convention here, and the most citable — quote the line and say what the POV
character would have to be able to do to know it.

Not a breach: an established omniscient or shifting POV. Check the book's declared POV
structure before flagging. That lives in the per-book conventions below.

### Stakes resting on something met once

A threat, a bond, or a loss carries weight the text has not bought. Name the stake, name the
single appearance it rests on, and give the distance between them.

## Default Conventions

Scene economy. Real exceptions exist, and a deliberate break is a choice rather than a
failure. Flag the break, say it reads as unintended and why, and stop there. When the writer
says it was deliberate, drop it for the session and record that in `context/`.

### Enter late, exit early

A scene starts at the last possible moment and ends at the first. Name the beats before the
real start and after the real end.

### Exit on the turn, not the settle

Sharper than the above. Cut at the moment the thing changes, not after the characters have
absorbed it. The aftermath beat is the usual offender.

### Every scene changes something

By the end, someone knows, wants, or can do something they could not at the start. When
nothing shifts, say so: *"this scene ends where it started."* Name the state at both ends.

### No scene does work another scene already did

Two scenes carrying the same beat. The second argument that relitigates the first, the second
revelation of a thing already revealed. Name both scenes and the beat they share.

### Because of, or in spite of — never and then

Every scene follows from an earlier one, as consequence or as reaction against it. Find the
scene this one is caused by or pushes back on. When you cannot find one, that missing
antecedent is the finding.

The finding is the missing antecedent, not the adjacency. Three cases are not breaches:

- The opening scene has nothing prior.
- A parallel plotline runs its causality to that thread's own last appearance, not to the
  scene physically before it.
- Two scenes placed together for contrast rather than consequence.

Search the whole preceding text for the antecedent before reporting one missing.

## Per-Book Conventions

The conventions above hold across everything the writer writes. A book's own rules — tense,
POV structure, chapter length, whether prologues are allowed — belong to that vault.

Read `_editor-notes/context/` for them, per `creative-vault`. When a book's rule contradicts
one here, the book wins, and say in one line that you are following it.

When a convention check needs a per-book rule that no context file records, say what you need
and let the writer answer. Do not assume the default.
